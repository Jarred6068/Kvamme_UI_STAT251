Week 9 Notes

STAT 251 Section 01

Finding normal probabilities

In Week 7 we said that for a continuous random variable, probability is area under the density curve, and that a single value has probability zero, \(P(X = c) = 0\). So the questions we ask about a continuous variable are always about an interval: below some value, above it, or between two values. For the normal distribution those areas come from the standard normal table (the \(z\)-table), which we first used in Week 6.

The catch is that the table gives only one kind of area: the area to the left of \(z\), \(P(Z \leq z)\), where \(Z \sim N(0, 1)\) is the standard normal. Every other area has to be built out of that one.

  • Lower tail. \(P(Z < z)\) is the table value itself.
  • Upper tail. The whole area under the curve is 1, so the area to the right is what is left over: \[P(Z > z) = 1 - P(Z \leq z)\]
  • Area between. Take everything to the left of the larger value, and remove everything to the left of the smaller one: \[P(a < Z < b) = P(Z \leq b) - P(Z \leq a)\]

Because \(P(Z = z) = 0\), it does not matter whether the inequality is \(<\) or \(\leq\). Always sketch the curve and shade the area first: the picture tells you which rule to use, and whether your answer should be big or small.

The lower tail

Example: lower tail

  1. \(P(Z < 1.25)\). Shade everything to the left of 1.25. Since 1.25 is above the mean of 0, the shaded area is most of the curve, so expect an answer well over one half. This is a lower tail, so the table value is the answer. Split \(1.25\) into \(1.2 + 0.05\): row 1.2, column .05 gives \(P(Z < 1.25) =\) 0.8944.

  2. \(P(Z < -0.84)\). Shade everything to the left of \(-0.84\). That is a small slice of the left side, less than one half, because a negative \(z\) is below the mean. It is again a lower tail: row \(-0.8\), column .04 gives \(P(Z < -0.84) =\) 0.2005.

The upper tail

Example: upper tail

  1. \(P(Z > 1.80)\). Shade everything to the right of 1.80, a thin tail, so expect a small answer. The table gives the area to the left, so look that up first: row 1.8, column .00 gives \(P(Z \leq 1.80) = 0.9641\). The whole area is 1, so \[P(Z > 1.80) = 1 - P(Z \leq 1.80) = 1 - 0.9641 = \mathbf{0.0359}\]

  2. \(P(Z > -1.30)\). Shade everything to the right of \(-1.30\), which is most of the curve. Row \(-1.3\), column .00 gives \(P(Z \leq -1.30) = 0.0968\), so \[P(Z > -1.30) = 1 - 0.0968 = \mathbf{0.9032}\]

The normal curve is symmetric, so the area to the right of \(-1.30\) is the same as the area to the left of \(+1.30\): the table gives \(P(Z \leq 1.30) = 0.9032\), the same answer.

The area between

Example: area between

  1. \(P(-1.30 < Z < 1.80)\). Shade the area between the two values. Take everything to the left of the larger value (row 1.8, column .00: 0.9641), and remove everything to the left of the smaller value (row \(-1.3\), column .00: 0.0968): \[P(-1.30 < Z < 1.80) = 0.9641 - 0.0968 = \mathbf{0.8673}\]

  2. \(P(-1.96 < Z < 1.96)\). Row 1.9, column .06 gives 0.9750, and row \(-1.9\), column .06 gives 0.0250: \[P(-1.96 < Z < 1.96) = 0.9750 - 0.0250 = \mathbf{0.9500}\]

Remember that second one. Exactly 95% of a normal distribution lies within 1.96 standard deviations of the mean; the empirical rule’s “2” is that number rounded. It is where the 2 in a 95% confidence interval comes from.

Practice

For \(Z \sim N(0, 1)\), sketch and shade each area, then use the table:

  1. \(P(Z \leq 2.00)\)   (b) \(P(Z > 0.50)\)   (c) \(P(-1 < Z < 1)\)   (d) \(P(-2 < Z < 2)\)

Worked answers:

    1. A lower tail, so the table value is the answer: row 2.0, column .00 gives \(P(Z \leq 2.00) = 0.9772\).
    1. An upper tail: row 0.5, column .00 gives \(P(Z \leq 0.50) = 0.6915\), so \(P(Z > 0.50) = 1 - 0.6915 = 0.3085\).
    1. An area between: row 1.0 gives 0.8413 and row \(-1.0\) gives 0.1587, so \(P(-1 < Z < 1) = 0.8413 - 0.1587 = 0.6826\), about 68%.
    1. An area between: row 2.0 gives 0.9772 and row \(-2.0\) gives 0.0228, so \(P(-2 < Z < 2) = 0.9772 - 0.0228 = 0.9544\), about 95%.
  1. and (d) are the empirical rule from Week 6: about 68% of a normal distribution lies within one standard deviation of the mean and about 95% within two.

Any normal distribution

The same three rules work for any normal variable \(X \sim N(\mu, \sigma)\): first convert each value to a \(z\)-score, \(z = (x - \mu)/\sigma\), then find the area for \(Z\). For example, if \(X \sim N(5, 10)\), then \(P(X \geq 25) = P(Z \geq 2) = 1 - 0.9772 = 0.0228\). The central limit theorem, next, uses exactly this, with the standard error in place of \(\sigma\).

The central limit theorem and an interval for a mean

This section works through every example from the Lecture 19 slides. The longer write-up, with the simulations behind the theorem, is on the Week 8 notes page, starting at “The central limit theorem”.

The theorem

In Week 8 we found the center and spread of the sampling distribution of \(\bar{x}\): mean \(\mu\) and standard error \(\sigma/\sqrt{n}\). The central limit theorem (CLT) gives its shape:

  • Central limit theorem. Take random samples of size \(n\) from any population with mean \(\mu\) and standard deviation \(\sigma\). If \(n\) is large enough, the sampling distribution of \(\bar{x}\) is approximately normal: \[\bar{x} \sim N\!\left(\mu,\; \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\] A sample proportion is a sample mean of 0s and 1s, so the same is true of \(\hat{p}\): \[\hat{p} \sim N\!\left(p,\; \sqrt{\frac{p(1 - p)}{n}}\right)\]

The surprising part is “any population”. Even when the population is skewed, like the minutes until the next goal at the World Cup, averages of enough observations are bell shaped. The larger \(n\) is, the better the approximation. The textbook’s guides for “large enough” are:

  • \(n \geq 30\) for \(\bar{x}\). A population that is close to normal needs less; one that is very skewed or has extreme outliers may need more.
  • \(np > 5\) and \(n(1 - p) > 5\) for \(\hat{p}\), so there are enough successes and failures.

Example: the CLT for a proportion

Stephen Curry has made 4,236 of his 4,645 career free throws, so take \(p = 0.912\). Let \(\hat{p}\) be the proportion he makes over a stretch of \(n\) free throws. Below are the exact sampling distributions of \(\hat{p}\) for \(n = 10\), 30 and 100. For which \(n\) is \(n(1 - p) > 5\), and which distributions look like a bell?

Curry almost always makes his free throws, so the check that can fail is the one for misses, \(n(1 - p)\), with \(1 - p = 0.088\):

  • \(n = 10\): \(10 \times 0.088 = 0.88\), which fails. The distribution is piled up against 1 and strongly skewed.
  • \(n = 30\): \(30 \times 0.088 = 2.64\), which still fails. It is still skewed left.
  • \(n = 100\): \(100 \times 0.088 = 8.8 > 5\), which passes. This is the only one close to a bell.

The check and the pictures agree. The CLT needs enough successes and enough failures; with too few expected misses, \(\hat{p}\) cannot spread out to the right of \(p\) and the distribution stays lopsided.

Using it

Because \(\bar{x}\) is approximately normal, we can find probabilities about it with the \(z\)-table, exactly as in the first part of these notes. The one change is that we standardize with the standard error, not \(\sigma\): \[z = \frac{\bar{x} - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}}\]

In particular, about 95% of the time a statistic lands within 2 standard errors of its parameter: \[P\left(\mu - 2\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \;\leq\; \bar{x} \;\leq\; \mu + 2\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\right) \approx 0.95\]

Example: free throws, again

Over a stretch of \(n = 100\) free throws, let \(\hat{p}\) be the proportion Curry makes, with \(p = 0.912\). We just checked that \(n(1 - p) = 8.8 > 5\) (and \(np = 91.2 > 5\)), so by the CLT \(\hat{p}\) is approximately normal.

  1. What are the mean and standard error of \(\hat{p}\)? The mean of \(\hat{p}\) is \(p\) itself, 0.912. The standard error is \[\sqrt{\frac{p(1 - p)}{n}} = \sqrt{\frac{0.912 \times 0.088}{100}} = \sqrt{0.000803} \approx \mathbf{0.028}\]

  2. About 95% of the time, \(\hat{p}\) falls between what two values? Within 2 standard errors of its mean: \[0.912 \pm 2(0.028) = 0.912 \pm 0.056, \quad \text{from } \mathbf{0.856} \text{ to } \mathbf{0.968}\] So in about 95% of stretches of 100 free throws, Curry makes between about 86 and 97 of them.

Example: a tournament’s average

The number of goals in a World Cup match is \(X \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda = 2.69)\), the 2022 rate. A tournament has 64 matches, so the tournament’s average number of goals per match is a sample mean with \(n = 64\). For a Poisson variable the mean and the variance are both \(\lambda\), so \[\mu = 2.69, \qquad \sigma = \sqrt{2.69} \approx 1.64\]

  1. What is the standard error of a tournament’s average? \[\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} = \frac{1.64}{\sqrt{64}} = \frac{1.64}{8} = \mathbf{0.205}\]

  2. About 95% of tournaments average between what two values? \(n = 64 \geq 30\), so the CLT says \(\bar{x}\) is approximately normal, and about 95% of tournaments land within 2 standard errors of \(\mu\): \[2.69 \pm 2(0.205) = 2.69 \pm 0.41, \quad \text{from } \mathbf{2.28} \text{ to } \mathbf{3.10} \text{ goals per match}\]

  3. How likely is a tournament that averages 3 or more goals per match? Sketch the normal curve for \(\bar{x}\) centered at 2.69 and shade the area to the right of 3, a small upper tail. Standardize with the standard error: \[z = \frac{3 - 2.69}{0.205} = \frac{0.31}{0.205} \approx 1.51\] Row 1.5, column .01 of the table gives \(P(Z \leq 1.51) = 0.9345\), so \[P(\bar{x} \geq 3) = P(Z \geq 1.51) = 1 - 0.9345 = \mathbf{0.0655}\] About a 7% chance. A single match can easily have 0 goals or 6; a tournament’s average is far more predictable.

A 95% confidence interval for a mean

If \(\bar{x}\) is within 2 standard errors of \(\mu\), then \(\mu\) is within 2 standard errors of \(\bar{x}\). Turning the statement around gives the 95% confidence interval for \(\mu\): \[\bar{x} \;\pm\; 2\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\] which has the form point estimate \(\pm\) margin of error.

These are the same songs as on the Week 8 page. A song credited to several artists appears once for each of them, so we count each song once, which leaves 18,860 different songs. One row is one song, and our variable is

Usually we see only a sample, but here we have every song, so we can treat the 18,860 songs as the population and compute its parameters directly: \[\mu = 3.71 \text{ minutes}, \qquad \sigma = 1.51 \text{ minutes}.\] Song length is skewed right: most songs run 2.5 to 5 minutes, but 41 are longer than 10 minutes (the Week 8 page has the histogram). Example: how often does an interval capture \(\mu\)?

We can check the method here, because we know \(\mu\). Below are 25 random samples of \(n = 20\) songs. For each one the standard error is \(\sigma/\sqrt{n} = 1.51/\sqrt{20} \approx 0.34\) minutes, and each line is \(\bar{x} \pm 2(0.34)\), the sample mean plus or minus the margin of error.

How many of the 25 intervals capture \(\mu = 3.71\)? Count the lines that cross the vertical line at 3.71: 24 of the 25 do, and one (in red) misses. That is 96%, close to the 95% we expect: about 95% of all samples put \(\bar{x}\) within 2 standard errors of \(\mu\), and exactly those samples give an interval that reaches \(\mu\). Each new set of 25 samples would give a slightly different count, but about 95% in the long run.

A confidence interval for a proportion

The interval for a mean was point estimate \(\pm\) 2 standard errors. The same idea works for a proportion. By the CLT, for large \(n\) the sample proportion \(\hat{p}\) is approximately normal with mean \(p\) and standard error \(\sqrt{p(1 - p)/n}\), so about 95% of samples give a \(\hat{p}\) within 2 standard errors of \(p\). Turning that around, as we did for the mean:

\[\hat{p} \;\pm\; 2\sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}(1 - \hat{p})}{n}}\]

is a 95% confidence interval for \(p\). There is one change from the formula for the standard error: we do not know \(p\) (that is what we are estimating), so the standard error uses \(\hat{p}\) in its place.

When it works. The interval leans on the CLT, so check that there are enough successes and failures in the sample: \(n\hat{p} > 5\) and \(n(1 - \hat{p}) > 5\).

Example: losing focus in class

Estimate \(p\), the proportion of all U.S. teens who lose focus in class sometimes or often.

  • Point estimate. \(178 + 60 = 238\) of the \(n = 743\) teens, so \(\hat{p} = 238/743 \approx 0.320\).
  • Check. There are 238 successes and 505 failures, both far more than 5.
  • Standard error. \(\sqrt{0.320 \times 0.680 / 743} \approx 0.017\).
  • Interval. \(0.320 \pm 2(0.017) = 0.320 \pm 0.034\), from 0.286 to 0.354.

We would report it as: “We estimate with 95% confidence that between 28.6% and 35.4% of U.S. teens lose focus in class sometimes or often by checking their phones.” As with the mean, the 95% describes the method: about 95% of samples of 743 teens would give an interval that captures \(p\).

Confidence levels and critical values

Every interval so far has been estimate \(\pm\) 2 standard errors, which captures the parameter about 95% of the time. Suppose a cardiologist wants to be 99% sure instead. Should the interval get wider or narrower? Wider: to catch the parameter more often, we need a bigger net. The thing that changes is the 2, which becomes a larger number.

In plain words

Think of the parameter (\(\mu\) or \(p\)) as a fish hidden somewhere in a murky pond. We cannot see the fish itself, only the ripples it makes, which tell us roughly where it is: that is our estimate, \(\bar{x}\) or \(\hat{p}\). A confidence interval is a net we throw around the estimate, hoping the fish is inside.

  • Confidence level. How often our way of throwing the net catches the fish. A 95% confidence level means that if we threw the net this way 100 times, from 100 different samples, about 95 throws would catch it.
  • \(\alpha\) (the error rate). How often the net misses: the risk of being wrong that we agree to live with. At 95% confidence, \(\alpha = 5\%\), so about 5 throws in 100 come up empty. Confidence level and \(\alpha\) always add up to 100%.
  • Critical value. How big the net is, measured in standard errors. To miss less often we need a bigger net: about 1.645 standard errors either side for 90%, 1.96 for 95% and 2.576 for 99%.
  • Precision. How narrow the net is. A narrow interval tells us a lot about where the fish is; a very wide one is almost certain to catch it but tells us little. A forecast that says “tomorrow’s high will be between \(-50\) and \(150\) degrees” is never wrong and never useful. A forecast of “between 71 and 72 degrees” is useful, but it will often be wrong.

That is the whole tension in this section: being sure (a high confidence level) and being precise (a narrow interval) pull against each other, and the sample size is what lets us have more of both.

In detail

  • Confidence level \(C\). The proportion of all possible samples whose interval captures the parameter, such as 90%, 95% or 99%. It is a property of the method, fixed before the data are gathered. Once one interval is computed, it either contains the parameter or it does not; the \(C\) tells us how often the method works in the long run.
  • \(\alpha\). The proportion of samples whose interval misses: \(\alpha = 1 - C\). A miss can happen on either side (the estimate lands too far above the parameter, or too far below), so \(\alpha\) is split evenly between the two tails of the normal curve, \(\alpha/2\) in each.
  • Critical value \(z_{1-\alpha/2}\). The \(z\) with area \(1 - \alpha/2\) to its left, so that the middle area between \(-z_{1-\alpha/2}\) and \(z_{1-\alpha/2}\) is exactly \(C\). A \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) confidence interval is \[\text{estimate} \;\pm\; z_{1-\alpha/2} \times SE\] For a mean this is \(\bar{x} \pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\sigma/\sqrt{n}\); for a proportion it is \(\hat{p} \pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})/n}\).
  • Margin of error \(m = z_{1-\alpha/2} \times SE\), the \(\pm\) part. The interval’s width is \(2m\), and a smaller width means a more precise estimate.

Where does the formula come from? By the central limit theorem, the estimate is approximately normal, centered at the parameter, with standard deviation \(SE\). So a proportion \(C\) of all samples give an estimate within \(z_{1-\alpha/2}\) standard errors of the parameter. For a mean, \[P\left(-z_{1-\alpha/2} \leq \frac{\bar{x} - \mu}{SE} \leq z_{1-\alpha/2}\right) = C.\] “\(\bar{x}\) is within \(z_{1-\alpha/2} \times SE\) of \(\mu\)” is the same statement as “\(\mu\) is within \(z_{1-\alpha/2} \times SE\) of \(\bar{x}\)”, so rearranging gives \[P\left(\bar{x} - z_{1-\alpha/2}\,SE \;\leq\; \mu \;\leq\; \bar{x} + z_{1-\alpha/2}\,SE\right) = C.\] The probability is about the random \(\bar{x}\), that is, about the method, before a sample is drawn. The “2” we used before is just \(z_{0.975} = 1.96\), rounded.

Why look up \(1 - \alpha/2\)? The table gives the area to the left of \(z\). To the left of the upper cut-off lies the middle \(C\) plus the lower tail \(\alpha/2\), which is \(1 - \alpha/2\) in all.

Example: critical values

Find the critical value for 80%, 90% and 99% confidence. For each one, find \(\alpha\), then look for the area \(1 - \alpha/2\) inside the \(z\)-table and read off \(z\) from the row and column:

  • 90%. \(\alpha = 0.10\), \(\alpha/2 = 0.05\), so look for 0.95. It sits exactly halfway between 0.9495 (\(z = 1.64\)) and 0.9505 (\(z = 1.65\)), so \(z_{0.95} = \mathbf{1.645}\).

An interval for a proportion at other confidence levels

The data: a gene and a drug

Clopidogrel (brand name Plavix) is a blood thinner given to people after a heart attack or when a stent is put in a heart artery. It is a prodrug: it does nothing until the liver enzyme CYP2C19 switches it on. The gene for that enzyme comes in several versions. People who carry the CYP2C19*2 variant (a single DNA letter change, G to A, at the site rs4244285) make a version of the enzyme that does not work, so they switch on less of the drug. For heart patients who carry one or two copies, the current clinical guideline (CPIC, 2022) recommends a different drug.

The 1000 Genomes Project sequenced the genomes of 2,504 people from 26 populations around the world to catalog human genetic variation. We use its 504 East Asian participants (Han Chinese in Beijing, Southern Han Chinese, Japanese in Tokyo, Kinh in Ho Chi Minh City, and Chinese Dai in Xishuangbanna). The first five:

HG00409 Southern Han Chinese G/G 0 0
… … … … …

Each person has two copies of the gene, one from each parent, so the genotype is two letters: G/G has no copies of *2, G/A has one and A/A has two.

Example: carriers of CYP2C19*2

Of the \(n = 504\) people, 239 carry one copy of *2 and 38 carry two, so 277 are carriers. Estimate \(p\), the proportion of all individuals of East Asian descent who carry the variant, with 90%, 95% and 99% confidence.

  1. Point estimate, check and standard error.
  • \(\hat{p} = 277 / 504 \approx 0.550\).
  • Check. There are 277 successes and 227 failures, both far more than 5.
  • Standard error. \(\sqrt{0.550 \times 0.450 / 504} = \sqrt{0.000491} \approx 0.022\).

99% 2.576 2.576(0.022) = 0.057 0.550 ± 0.057: 0.493 to 0.607

For example, the 90% interval is reported as: “We estimate with 90% confidence that between 51.4% and 58.6% of individuals of East Asian descent carry at least one copy of CYP2C19*2.”

  1. Can we say that more than half of individuals of East Asian descent carry *2? At 90% and 95% confidence, yes: every value in those intervals is above 0.5. At 99% confidence, no: the interval reaches down to 0.493, so 0.5 is still a plausible value. Asking for more confidence costs precision.

Width, confidence level and sample size

The margin of error is \(z_{1-\alpha/2} \times SE\), so two things control the width of an interval: the confidence level, through \(z\), and the sample size, through the standard error. The plot shows the carrier interval at four confidence levels (left), and the 95% interval as it would be with 4 times fewer or 4 times more people at the same \(\hat{p}\) (right).

  • Higher confidence, wider interval. A bigger \(C\) means a bigger critical value.
  • Larger sample, narrower interval. The standard error has \(\sqrt{n}\) on the bottom, so 4 times the sample size gives half the width.

Two dials: in plain words

Picture the interval’s width controlled by two dials.

  • The confidence dial. Turn it up and the net gets bigger, so it misses less often, but the interval gets wider and says less about where the parameter is. Turn it down and the interval gets narrower, but it misses more often. This dial only trades one thing for another: sureness for precision. It never gives you more of both.
  • The sample size dial. Turn it up (collect more data) and the estimate itself gets steadier, so the same size of net, in standard errors, covers less ground. The interval gets narrower without losing any confidence. This is the only dial that buys precision for free. Well, not quite free: more data costs time and money.

A third thing, the spread of the population (\(\sigma\)), also affects the width, but it is not a dial: it is a fact about the population that we cannot change.

Two dials: in detail

The width of an interval is \[\text{width} = 2m = 2 \times z_{1-\alpha/2} \times SE, \qquad SE = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \text{ or } \sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}.\] The confidence level enters only through \(z_{1-\alpha/2}\), and the sample size only through \(SE\):

Dial Setting Critical value SE Width, 2 × z × SE Intervals that miss
Confidence level 90% 1.645 0.022 0.072 10%
95% 1.960 0.022 0.086 5%
99% 2.576 0.022 0.114 1%
Sample size (at 95%) n = 126 1.960 0.044 0.172 5%
n = 504 1.960 0.022 0.086 5%
n = 2,016 1.960 0.011 0.044 5%

The table uses the CYP2C19 carriers (\(\hat{p} = 0.550\), \(n = 504\), \(SE = 0.022\)).

  • Turning up the confidence level from 90% to 99% raises \(z\) from 1.645 to 2.576, so the width grows by a factor of \(2.576/1.645 \approx 1.6\), from 0.072 to 0.114. The miss rate \(\alpha\) drops from 10% to 1%. Same data, so no new information: we have traded precision for confidence.
  • Turning up the sample size 4 times, from 504 to 2,016 people, cuts the \(SE\) in half (\(\sqrt{4} = 2\)), so the width halves, from 0.086 to 0.044 (0.043 before rounding), while the miss rate stays at 5%. Precision improves at no cost in confidence. Because of the square root, though, each halving of the width costs 4 times as many observations, so precision gets expensive fast.

Precision is not accuracy. Recall the bullseye: the standard error measures precision (how tightly estimates cluster), and bias measures accuracy (whether they center on the target). Both dials work only on precision and on the random error from sampling. A biased sample, such as a voluntary-response survey, gives a narrow interval around the wrong value, and no confidence level or sample size fixes that.

Would you board this plane?

Lecture 21 opened with this discussion: here is why the confidence level matters outside a classroom. An airplane maker has a new passenger jet and is starting to sell it to airlines. Before it enters wide service, its engineers run a study to estimate the probability of a catastrophic failure in flight, and they report that it is between 0.00012 and 0.0054 per flight. Your flight to the Bahamas turns out to be on one of these planes. (This study is made up; the airline numbers below are real.)

  1. Would you feel comfortable flying on this plane? Turn the interval into flights: \(1/0.00012 \approx 8,333\) and \(1/0.0054 \approx 185\), so the study says somewhere between 1 failure in 8,300 flights and 1 in 185 flights. For comparison, the world’s airlines had 1 fatal accident per 5.6 million flights on average over 2021–2025 (IATA). Even the best case in this interval is about \(0.00012 \times 5{,}600{,}000 \approx 672\) times riskier than a typical flight. Most of us would stay home.

  2. If the maker could report this interval at 90%, 95% or 99% confidence, which would you want? For a safety decision the number that matters is the upper end, the worst case that the data still allow. A 90% interval is the narrowest, but \(\alpha = 0.10\): 1 in 10 intervals built this way misses, and when it misses, the true risk could be above its upper end. A 99% interval is wider, so its upper end is higher and more cautious, but only 1 in 100 such intervals misses. When being wrong is costly, we want high confidence, and we accept a wider interval to get it.

  3. Would you feel differently if the interval were narrower but had a lower confidence level? It should not reassure you. From the same data, a narrower interval only comes from a smaller critical value, which means a lower confidence level: it looks more precise but is more likely to be wrong. The only way to make the interval narrower and keep the confidence is to collect more data, a larger \(n\).

Intervals for a mean when \(\sigma\) is unknown

Every interval for \(\mu\) so far used \(\sigma\), the population standard deviation: \[\bar{x} \pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\] But if we do not know \(\mu\), we almost never know \(\sigma\) either. The natural fix is to put the sample standard deviation \(s\) in its place: \[\bar{x} \pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}\] Unfortunately, this interval captures \(\mu\) less often than the confidence level promises, especially when \(n\) is small. The reason is that \(s\) itself changes from sample to sample. When a sample happens to have a small \(s\), its interval is too narrow, and those narrow intervals miss \(\mu\) more often.

Does \(s\) in place of \(\sigma\) work?

Suppose seal whisker lengths are normal with \(\mu = 91\) mm and \(\sigma = 8\) mm (made-up values, close to the spotted seals below). We simulate 100 samples of only \(n = 5\) whiskers each, and from each one build the interval \(\bar{x} \pm 1.96\,s/\sqrt{5}\). A 95% method should miss about 5 times. The top panel shows the result; misses are in red.

With 1.96, 12 of the 100 intervals miss \(\mu\), more than twice the 5 we expect. (In the long run this method captures \(\mu\) only about 88% of the time when \(n = 5\).) The bottom panel uses the same 100 samples with a larger multiplier, 2.776, and 5 of the 100 miss, just as a 95% method should. That larger multiplier comes from the \(t\) distribution.

The \(t\) distribution

When we standardize \(\bar{x}\) with \(s\) instead of \(\sigma\), the result \[t = \frac{\bar{x} - \mu}{s/\sqrt{n}}\] does not follow the standard normal distribution. It follows a \(t\) distribution.

  • \(t\) distribution. A bell-shaped curve centered at 0, like \(N(0, 1)\), but with heavier tails: more of its area is far from 0. It accounts for the extra uncertainty from estimating \(\sigma\) with \(s\).
  • Degrees of freedom (\(df\)). The \(t\) distribution is a family of curves, one for each value of \(df = n - 1\). The more degrees of freedom, the closer \(t\) is to \(N(0, 1)\).
  • \(t\) critical value \(t_{n-1,\,1-\alpha/2}\). The value with area \(1 - \alpha/2\) to its left under the \(t\) distribution with \(n - 1\) degrees of freedom, read from the \(t\)-table.

As \(n\) grows, \(s\) becomes a more and more reliable estimate of \(\sigma\), and the \(t\) distribution approaches the standard normal. By about \(df = 30\) the two are nearly indistinguishable, and a \(t\) distribution with infinitely many degrees of freedom is the standard normal.

The interval for \(\mu\) when \(\sigma\) is unknown is \[\bar{x} \;\pm\; t_{n-1,\,1-\alpha/2}\,\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}\] Strictly, it assumes the population is normal, but it works well for most populations as long as they are not very skewed or full of outliers, and for any population once \(n \geq 30\).

Which interval?

Estimating Interval When it works
a proportion \(p\) \(\hat{p} \pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})/n}\) \(n\hat{p} > 5\) and \(n(1-\hat{p}) > 5\)
a mean \(\mu\), \(\sigma\) known \(\bar{x} \pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\sigma/\sqrt{n}\) the population is normal, or \(n \geq 30\)
a mean \(\mu\), \(\sigma\) unknown \(\bar{x} \pm t_{n-1,\,1-\alpha/2}\,s/\sqrt{n}\) the population is roughly normal, or \(n \geq 30\)

The interval for a proportion always uses \(z\): there is no separate \(\sigma\) to estimate, because the standard error comes from \(\hat{p}\) itself.

Working the example

Estimate \(\mu\), the mean whisker length of all spotted seals, with 95% confidence, from the 9 that were measured: \(n = 9\), \(\bar{x} = 91.2\) mm, \(s = 7.9\) mm.

  1. \(z\) or \(t\)? How many degrees of freedom? We know only \(s\), not \(\sigma\), so use \(t\), with \(df = 9 - 1 = 8\). Row 8, column 95% of the \(t\)-table gives \(t = 2.306\).

  2. What is the standard error? \[\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}} = \frac{7.9}{\sqrt{9}} = \frac{7.9}{3} \approx 2.63 \text{ mm}\]

  3. What is the 95% confidence interval for \(\mu\)? \[91.2 \pm 2.306(2.63) = 91.2 \pm 6.06, \quad \text{from } \mathbf{85.1} \text{ to } \mathbf{97.3} \text{ mm}\]

“We estimate with 95% confidence that the mean length of a spotted seal’s longest whisker is between 85.1 and 97.3 mm.” With \(z = 1.96\) instead, the margin of error would have been only \(1.96(2.63) = 5.15\) mm, too narrow for a sample of 9.

Choosing a minimum sample size

The margin of error \(m\) is the \(\pm\) part of an interval. It shrinks when the confidence level goes down or when \(n\) goes up. We rarely want to give up confidence, and we cannot change how spread out the population is, but before a study we can choose \(n\). Real studies cost time and money, so the question is: what is the smallest \(n\) that gives the margin of error we need?

Solve the margin-of-error formula for \(n\):

  • For a proportion: \(m = z\sqrt{\dfrac{p(1-p)}{n}}\), so \[n = \frac{z^2\, p^*(1-p^*)}{m^2}\] where \(p^*\) is a guess at \(p\) from an earlier study. With no earlier study, use \(p^* = 0.5\): the product \(p(1-p)\) is largest at \(p = 0.5\), so this gives a sample big enough whatever \(p\) turns out to be.
  • For a mean: \(m = z\dfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\), so \[n = \left(\frac{z\,\sigma}{m}\right)^2\] where \(\sigma\) comes from an earlier study. With nothing to go on, a rough guess is \(\sigma \approx \text{range}/4\), because most of a roughly normal population lies within 2 standard deviations of the mean.
  • Always round up. A sample of 59 would give a margin of error slightly larger than the target; 60 does not.

We use \(z\) in both formulas, even for a mean: the \(t\) critical value depends on \(n\), which is what we are trying to find.

Example: planning the next study

  1. How many people would a new study need to estimate the proportion of CYP2C19*2 carriers within \(\pm 0.03\) with 95% confidence, if we had no earlier guess? Use \(p^* = 0.5\) and \(z = 1.96\): \[n = \frac{1.96^2(0.5)(0.5)}{0.03^2} = \frac{3.8416 \times 0.25}{0.0009} = 1067.1 \quad\to\quad \mathbf{1{,}068} \text{ people}\] With the earlier estimate \(p^* = 0.55\) instead, \(n = 1.96^2(0.55)(0.45)/0.03^2 = 1056.4\), so 1,057 people: a little smaller, because 0.55 is not 0.5.

  2. How many spotted seals would we need to estimate the mean whisker length within \(\pm 2\) mm with 95% confidence? Use \(\sigma \approx 7.9\) mm from Ginter et al.: \[n = \left(\frac{1.96 \times 7.9}{2}\right)^2 = (7.742)^2 = 59.9 \quad\to\quad \mathbf{60} \text{ seals}\]

  3. What happens to \(n\) if we want half the margin of error? \(m\) is squared on the bottom, so halving \(m\) makes \(n\) 4 times as large. For the carriers, \(\pm 0.015\) needs 4,269 people instead of 1,068.

    • (The Lecture 22 practice problem.) Campus dining wants to estimate the proportion of students who skip breakfast, within \(\pm 0.04\) with 90% confidence. Last year’s survey found that 30% skip breakfast. (a) How many students should they survey, using last year’s 30%? (b) How many if there were no earlier survey? (c) Why is (b) larger? Answer: (a) \(n = 1.645^2(0.3)(0.7)/0.04^2 = 355.2\), so 356 students. (b) With \(p^* = 0.5\), \(n = 1.645^2(0.5)(0.5)/0.04^2 = 422.8\), so 423 students. (c) \(p^*(1-p^*)\) is largest at \(p^* = 0.5\), so having no earlier guess is the safe, larger choice.
    • The sensitivity of a medical test is the probability that it comes back positive when the person has the disease. Rapid strep tests have a sensitivity of about 0.95. How many tests of a new rapid strep test would be needed to estimate its sensitivity within \(\pm 0.02\) with 95% confidence? Answer: \(n = 1.96^2(0.95)(0.05)/0.02^2 = 456.2\), so 457 tests.
    • A researcher wants to estimate the mean number of hours college students spend on TikTok each week, within \(\pm 0.5\) hours with 95% confidence. Earlier studies suggest \(\sigma \approx 2.5\) hours. Answer: \(n = (1.96 \times 2.5/0.5)^2 = 96.04\), so 97 students.

Assumptions behind these intervals

Every interval in these notes rests on a few assumptions:

  • The data are a random sample from the population (an unbiased sampling design; see Weeks 4 and 5).
  • The population is much larger than the sample. For a small, finite population the formulas change.
  • The sampling distribution is roughly normal, which needs one of:
    • for \(\hat{p}\): at least 5 successes and 5 failures, \(n\hat{p} > 5\) and \(n(1-\hat{p}) > 5\);
    • for \(\bar{x}\): a roughly normal population, or a large sample, \(n \geq 30\) (the central limit theorem). Plot the data to check for strong skew or outliers.

No formula fixes a biased sample: a margin of error measures only the random error from sampling.
