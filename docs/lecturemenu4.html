Week 4 Notes

STAT 251 Section 01

Week 3 finished our tour of descriptive statistics: we can now describe the shape of a distribution, its center, its spread, and the position of any individual value within it. Everything on Exam 1 comes from that material.

This week starts a different question, and it is one that logically comes first: before we trust any of those summaries to tell us something beyond the numbers in front of us, how were the data collected? Two questions decide what a set of data is allowed to tell us:

  • Can we generalize from the sample to a whole population? That depends on the sampling design — the first half of this lecture.
  • Can we say that one variable causes a change in another? That depends on the experimental design — the second half.

Sampling designs

A question to start

How many hours a week does a typical University of Idaho student spend exercising? We cannot ask every student. So how would you get the data — who would you ask, and how would you choose them? Keep your answer in mind; the rest of this section is about what makes one answer better than another.

The language of sampling

  • The population is the entire group we want to learn about — here, every UI student.
  • A parameter is a number that describes the population, such as the population mean \(\mu\) or proportion \(p\) — here, the mean weekly exercise hours of all UI students.
  • The sample is the part of the population we actually observe — the students we end up asking.
  • A statistic is a number computed from the sample, such as \(\bar{x}\) or \(\hat{p}\) — the mean hours of the students we asked.
  • A census collects data on every member of the population. It is usually slow, expensive, or impossible.
  • The sampling frame is the list of units we actually draw the sample from — for example, the registrar’s list of enrolled students. It is rarely a perfect copy of the population.
  • The sampling design is the rule used to choose the sample from the frame.
  • A representative sample has the same characteristics as the population it comes from. Producing one is the goal of every sampling design.
  • Sampling error is the gap between a statistic and the parameter it estimates that comes purely from which units happened to be chosen. A different sample would give a slightly different answer.

Example: census, frame and sampling error

Picture a small school of 100 students, and the question “how many hours did you exercise last week?” A census asks all 100 of them; their mean, 4.6 hours, is the parameter. The registrar’s list is the sampling frame, and it holds only 90 students — the 10 who enrolled late (grey) cannot be chosen. A simple random sample of 10 from the list (red) has a mean of 4.4 hours; that is the statistic. The sampling error is 4.4 − 4.6 = -0.2 hours, and a new random sample would give a different one.

Practice: find the sampling frame

  1. Student government wants to know how students feel about a parking-fee increase. It emails a survey to 500 students picked at random from the university email directory. Frame: the email directory — it lists nearly every enrolled student, so it matches the population well.
  2. A Moscow coffee shop wants its customers’ opinions on a new menu. It surveys 100 people picked at random from its loyalty-app members. Frame: the loyalty-app member list — customers who never joined the app cannot be chosen.
  3. A city council wants residents’ views on a new bike lane. It calls numbers picked at random from a printed phone directory. Frame: the phone directory — it misses everyone without a listed landline, a group that skews young.

Be skeptical

Before trusting any study, ask the questions from the textbook’s Critical Evaluation list (Section 1.2):

  • Problems with samples. Is the sample representative of the population? A biased sample gives results that are inaccurate and not valid.
  • Self-selected samples. Responses only from people who chose to respond, such as call-in or online polls, are often unreliable.
  • Sample size. Samples that are too small may be unreliable. Larger samples are better when possible, though sometimes a small sample is unavoidable (crash tests, rare conditions).
  • Undue influence. Were the data collected, or the questions asked, in a way that pushes people toward an answer?
  • Nonresponse. If many people refused, the responses may not represent the population; people with strong opinions are the most likely to answer.
  • Causality. A relationship between two variables does not mean one causes the other.
  • Self-funded or self-interest studies. A study paid for by someone who wants a particular answer deserves careful reading — but judge it on its merits, not only on who paid.
  • Misleading use of data. Watch for badly displayed graphs, incomplete data, or numbers without context.
  • Confounding. When the effects of several factors on the response cannot be separated, it is hard or impossible to draw conclusions about any one of them.

The rest of this lecture fills in most of these: bias and self-selection next, nonresponse and question wording later, then causality and confounding in the second half.

Examples: misleading graphs

OxyContin. Purdue promoted OxyContin, a 12-hour opioid painkiller, with a chart of blood levels over time captioned “Fewer ‘peaks and valleys’ than with immediate-release oxycodone” — drawn on a logarithmic y-axis (CBS News, 2011). On a log axis, equal distances mean equal ratios, so a steep fall can look gentle. The figure below is an illustration, not Purdue’s data: one curve matched to the 2008 FDA label’s values for a 10 mg dose (a peak of 10.6 ng/mL at about 2.7 hours, and a 4.5-hour half-life), drawn on both kinds of axis.

On the linear axis the level falls from 10.6 to 3.2 ng/mL — about 70% — over the 12 hours. In 2007 The Purdue Frederick Company pleaded guilty to misbranding OxyContin “with the intent to defraud and mislead”; the government’s statement of facts says sales staff used “graphs that exaggerated the differences between blood plasma levels” of OxyContin and other pain medications. The company paid about $600 million (U.S. Attorney, Western District of Virginia, 2007). A log axis is not wrong in itself — scientists use them all the time. The problem is using one to support a claim that a reader cannot check.

ExxonMobil. A company’s own report deserves the same scrutiny as anyone else’s, especially when it has a financial stake in the answer. ExxonMobil’s 2024 Advancing Climate Solutions report charts a roadmap for cutting its emissions intensity (emissions per unit produced) as bars with no numbers on the y-axis, and its abatement curve has no axis numbers either, so a reader cannot tell how big the planned cut is or how much of it rests on “CCS, hydrogen, and/or future advancements” (Union of Concerned Scientists, 2024). Intensity can fall while total emissions rise, if output grows. In this case the report’s own table shows both falling: intensity from 26.4 to 23.3 tonnes per 100 tonnes of throughput or production, and emissions from its operations (Scope 1 and 2) from 117 to 98 million tonnes, 2016 to 2023. What neither number covers is the emissions from burning the oil and gas the company produces — about 540 million tonnes in 2023, roughly 85% of its footprint, for which it sets no target (ExxonMobil, 2024).

Two easy ways to get a sample

Two designs are tempting because they are quick, cheap and easy:

  • A convenience sample asks whoever is easiest to reach — for instance, standing outside the Student Recreation Center and asking the first 50 people who walk by.
  • A voluntary response sample puts out a call and lets people choose whether to answer — for instance, posting a poll on a UI student Instagram account and counting the replies. The textbook calls these self-selected samples.

What could go wrong?

  • The Rec Center survey’s average will be too high: the people at the Rec Center are the ones who exercise, and students who never go there cannot be chosen at all.
  • The Instagram poll hears only from followers who see the post and choose to reply — often the proudest gym-goers, or people with strong feelings either way.
  • Asking 2,000 people instead of 50 fixes neither problem. More of the same kind of people gives the same wrong answer, just more confidently.

What the two designs have in common is that a person decides who is in the sample: the researcher picks whoever is handy, or the respondents pick themselves. A design like that is biased — it systematically favors some outcomes over others.

Practice: convenience or voluntary response?

For each story, name the kind of sample and what could go wrong.

  1. A nutrition club wants to know how many servings of vegetables UI students eat a day. Members ask the first 60 students who walk out of the campus dining hall at lunch. Convenience sample. Only lunchtime dining-hall eaters can be chosen — off-campus students and students who cook for themselves are missed.
  2. The campus paper wants to know whether students favor moving 8 a.m. classes later. It posts a “Vote now!” link on social media; 1,200 students vote and 85% say yes. Voluntary response sample. Students who dread 8 a.m. classes are the most eager to vote, so 85% likely overstates support — and 1,200 votes do not fix that.
  3. A professor wants to know how many hours a week the 300 students in her lecture study. She asks the 25 students who come to her office hours that week. Convenience sample — just whoever was there. Students who come to office hours probably study more than the class as a whole, so the estimate is likely too high.

Example: 17.5 million votes

On December 18, 2022, Elon Musk posted a poll on Twitter: “Should I step down as head of Twitter? I will abide by the results of this poll.” In 12 hours it drew more than 17.5 million votes, and 57.5% said yes (NBC News; Associated Press). The only people who could vote were those who saw the post and chose to — mostly Musk’s own followers — and nothing prevented fake accounts from voting. It is a voluntary response sample, and 17.5 million self-selected votes describe only the people who chose to vote. The size of the sample does not fix that.

Example: online opt-in polls

Many online polls draw on opt-in panels: anyone can sign up to take surveys, and they are paid for each one they complete. In opt-in samples, 12% of adults under 30 — and 24% of Hispanic respondents — said they were licensed to operate a nuclear submarine. An opt-in poll in December 2023 found that 20% of adults under 30 agreed that “the Holocaust is a myth”; when Pew Research Center asked the same question of a probability-based (random) sample, the figure was 3% (Mercer, Kennedy and Keeter, 2024). Bogus and careless respondents answering for the money, not real opinions, produced most of the difference. A shocking number about a subgroup can be a fact about who signed up, rather than about what people think.

Bias and standard error: shots at a target

Think of the parameter as the bullseye of a target, and of each estimate as one shot: take a sample, compute the statistic, and see where it lands. Take a new sample and you take a new shot. Two different things can go wrong.

  • Bias is an accuracy problem: the shots are centered away from the bullseye, missing in the same direction every time. It comes from the design — from who gets into the sample — and it does not average out.
  • Standard error is a precision problem: how spread out the shots are from one sample to the next. You met it in the Week 3 notes as \(SE(\bar{x}) = s/\sqrt{n}\), and it shrinks as the sample gets bigger.

Convenience and voluntary response samples are biased: however carefully they are carried out, they are aimed at the wrong spot.

A bigger sample does not fix bias

The simulation below makes the point. Suppose 40% of UI students exercise at least three times a week. One survey asks \(n = 2000\) people at the Student Recreation Center, where 75% do; the other is a simple random sample of just \(n = 200\) students. Each survey is repeated 5000 times.

The Rec Center survey is far more precise — its estimates barely move from one repeat to the next — and every one of its 5000 estimates misses the truth by about 35 percentage points. More data buys precision; only a better design buys accuracy.

The fix: let chance choose

Convenience and voluntary response samples go wrong because a person decides who gets in, so the fix is to take the person out of it. Random sampling uses chance to choose the sample, so that neither the researcher nor the respondents decide who is in it. Because no one is doing the choosing, the selection itself cannot systematically favor anyone. Chance still moves the estimate from sample to sample — but by rules we understand, so we can measure how much. That is exactly what the standard error does.

Simple random sampling

A simple random sample (SRS) of size \(n\) is chosen so that every possible group of \(n\) units has the same chance of being the sample. As a consequence, every unit in a population of size \(N\) has the same chance, \(n/N\), of being included.

The grid pictures on this page all use the same population: \(N = 100\) students on a \(10 \times 10\) grid, where each row is a class year (40 first-years, 30 sophomores, 20 juniors, 10 seniors). Every design draws \(n = 20\), so each student in the SRS below had a \(20/100 = 1\) in 5 chance of being chosen.

Look at the class years of the red students: this SRS happened to draw 4 seniors but only 2 juniors, even though there are twice as many juniors. That is not a flaw. An SRS is fair on average over all the samples it could have produced, not balanced every time.

Drawing an SRS in practice

  1. Get a sampling frame and number its units \(1\) to \(N\).
  2. Let a random number generator choose \(n\) different numbers.
  3. The units with those numbers are the sample.

In R, sample() does step 2. For the heights.csv data on the course Data page (\(N = 379\)):

heights <- read.csv('Data/heights.csv')
set.seed(251)                              # makes the draw repeatable
srs_rows <- sample(1:nrow(heights), 30)    # 30 of the 379 row numbers
head(heights[srs_rows, ])
##     HEIGHT GENDER
## 348     73   Male
## 291     69   Male
## 48      63 Female
## 339     72   Male
## 238     69 Female
## 59      63 Female

sample() draws without replacement by default: a unit that has been picked cannot be picked again, which is the usual choice for surveys. Sampling with replacement puts every unit back after it is drawn, so the same unit could appear twice (sample(..., replace = TRUE)). When \(N\) is much larger than \(n\) the two are practically the same, because the chance of drawing the same unit twice is tiny.

Above: without replacement, each ball that is drawn leaves the bag, so the sample 3, 1, 5 can have no repeats. With replacement, every ball goes back before the next draw, so 3 can be drawn twice.

Systematic sampling

A systematic sample takes every \(k\)th unit from an ordered list, starting at a randomly chosen point among the first \(k\). With \(N = 100\) and \(n = 20\), \(k = N/n = 5\). Below, the 100 students are numbered in list order; the random start was 2, so the sample is positions 2, 7, 12, 17, …, 97.

Systematic sampling is easy to carry out in the field, where there is often no list at all until units arrive. It behaves like an SRS as long as the order of the list has nothing to do with what is being measured. The danger is a list with a repeating pattern that lines up with \(k\): on our seating grid of rows of 10, counting every 5th seat would select the same two columns every time.

For a project dataset of \(N = 379\) rows and \(n = 30\): \(379/30 = 12.6\), so take \(k = 12\) and a random start between 1 and 12.

set.seed(251)
k <- 12
start <- sample(1:k, 1)
sys_rows <- seq(start, by = k, length.out = 30)
sys_rows
##  [1]   7  19  31  43  55  67  79  91 103 115 127 139 151 163 175 187 199 211 223
## [20] 235 247 259 271 283 295 307 319 331 343 355

Stratified random sampling

Strata are groups of units that are alike in some way, formed before sampling: class year, region, age group, sex. A stratified random sample takes a separate SRS inside every stratum and combines them.

Under proportional allocation, each stratum gets its share of \(n\). Our population is 40% first-years, 30% sophomores, 20% juniors and 10% seniors, so a sample of 20 takes 8, 6, 4 and 2.

Stratifying guarantees that every group is represented — no more 2 juniors by bad luck. It also gives more precise estimates than an SRS of the same size when units are alike within strata and differ between them, because the stratum-to-stratum variation is removed from the sampling error. Strata that are no more alike inside than the population as a whole buy nothing.

Allocation with real data

heights.csv has \(N = 379\) people: 262 female and 117 male. For a stratified sample of \(n = 30\):

\[\text{female: } 30 \times \frac{262}{379} = 20.74 \approx 21, \qquad \text{male: } 30 \times \frac{117}{379} = 9.26 \approx 9\]

Then draw an SRS of 21 of the women and a separate SRS of 9 of the men:

set.seed(251)
women <- which(heights$GENDER == 'Female')
men   <- which(heights$GENDER == 'Male')
strat_rows <- c(sample(women, 21), sample(men, 9))
table(heights$GENDER[strat_rows])
## 
## Female   Male 
##     21      9

Large national surveys are stratified. The Current Population Survey, which produces the U.S. unemployment rate, groups areas into strata “as homogeneous as possible with respect to labor force and other social and economic characteristics” before sampling within each (Census Bureau and BLS, Technical Paper 77).

Cluster sampling

A cluster is a group of units that occurs naturally: a dorm, a classroom, a city block, a village. A cluster sample takes an SRS of clusters and then measures every unit in the clusters chosen. Below, each column is a cluster; two columns were chosen at random.

The appeal is practical. We need a list of the clusters, not of every individual, and visiting a few clusters is far cheaper than travelling to units scattered across a whole region. Cluster sampling works best when each cluster is a mini-population — mixed inside, the way every column above contains all four class years. When units within a cluster resemble each other (students in the same dorm, households on the same street), a cluster sample carries less information than an SRS of the same size, and its estimates are less precise.

Two-stage cluster sampling

A two-stage cluster sample takes an SRS of clusters (stage 1) and then an SRS of units inside each chosen cluster (stage 2). Below: 4 of the 10 columns, then 5 students in each.

Election exit polls combine several designs. For the 2024 election, the National Election Pool drew a stratified probability sample of 279 polling places, and within each one an interviewer approached every \(n\)th voter leaving — a systematic sample inside each cluster (National Election Pool methods statement, 2024). NHANES, the U.S. health and nutrition survey, uses “a complex, multistage, probability sampling design”: counties, then segments within counties, then households, then individuals (CDC, NHANES sample design).

Strata versus clusters

The two are easy to confuse, because both divide the population into groups. The difference is what the groups look like inside, and so how many of them you sample:

Strata Clusters
Units inside a group are… alike mixed (a mini-population)
Groups differ from each other? yes ideally not
Which groups are sampled? every stratum some clusters
Within a chosen group… an SRS every unit (or an SRS, in two stages)
Main benefit precision, and every group represented cost and convenience

Two kinds of error

No sample matches its population exactly, and it helps to separate two reasons why:

  • Sampling error is the natural gap between a sample and the population, caused by which units happened to be chosen. Every sample has some, and a larger sample makes it smaller.
  • Nonsampling error is error that does not come from chance: biased sampling methods, badly worded questions, data-entry mistakes, a faulty measuring instrument. A larger sample does not make it smaller.
  • Sampling bias occurs when some members of the population are less likely to be chosen than others — convenience and voluntary response samples, or a sampling frame that leaves people out.

Three common nonsampling errors

Even a perfectly random design can produce a biased sample:

  • Undercoverage: the frame leaves part of the population out — students who are not on the list you sampled from, people with no phone or no fixed address.
  • Nonresponse: people who are chosen cannot be reached, or refuse to answer.
  • Response bias: the answers themselves are wrong, because of leading or confusing questions, sensitive topics, or respondents wanting to please the interviewer.

A larger sample fixes none of these.

Example: nonresponse

Pew Research Center’s telephone surveys reached 36% of the people they called in 1997, and only 6% in 2018 (Kennedy and Hartig, 2019). A low response rate does not by itself make a survey biased; it causes bias only when the people who answer differ from the people who do not — and that is usually impossible to check, because we have no data on the people who did not answer.

In the 2020 presidential election, national polls in the final two weeks overstated Joe Biden’s margin by 3.9 points on average, the largest polling error for the national popular vote in 40 years. The American Association for Public Opinion Research’s task force ruled out several explanations, such as late-deciding voters, and concluded that “at least some of the polling error in 2020 was caused by unit nonresponse” — but that without knowing how the people who did not respond compare with those who did, the primary source cannot be pinned down (AAPOR Task Force on 2020 Pre-Election Polling).

Example: question wording

In September 2013, CNBC asked half of its poll respondents about “Obamacare” and the other half about “the Affordable Care Act” — the same law. Opposition was 46% for “Obamacare” and 37% for “the Affordable Care Act.” Support was also higher for “Obamacare,” 29% versus 22%, because far fewer people chose “don’t know”: 12% for “Obamacare” against 30% for the Affordable Care Act (NBC News, 2013; Harvard Political Review). The words in a question change the answers.

Wording can also change what a question measures. In January 2003, 68% of Americans favored “taking military action in Iraq to end Saddam Hussein’s rule”; when the question added “even if it meant that U.S. forces might suffer thousands of casualties,” support fell to 43% (Pew Research Center, Writing Survey Questions).

Choosing a design for your course project

Part 3 of the course project asks you to draw a sample of 20 to 30 observations from your dataset. When you write it up:

  • choose a design — simple random, systematic, stratified or cluster sampling;
  • say exactly how you drew it (the method, \(n\), and the tool, such as the R code above) so that someone else could repeat it;
  • justify the choice for your data: is there a natural group worth stratifying on? are there natural clusters?
  • name any bias your method could introduce.

Steps 1 to 4 of the project can be started once this lecture is finished.

For practice before the project, try the textbook’s Sampling Experiment lab (Section 1.6): it lists restaurants by city and by entree cost and asks you to draw a simple random, a systematic, two stratified, and a cluster sample from the same list. The textbook’s Section 1.2 examples (Examples 1.11 to 1.13) are good practice at naming sampling designs.

Experiments and causation

Observational studies and experiments

  • The response variable is the outcome we measure.
  • The explanatory variable is the variable we think explains or influences the response.
  • An observational study observes individuals and measures variables of interest without influencing anything.
  • An experiment deliberately assigns treatments to the units, then measures the response.

The question that tells them apart is who decided which group each unit ended up in? If the units themselves (or circumstance) decided, it is an observational study. If the researcher decided, it is an experiment. Note that this has nothing to do with how the units were sampled: an experiment on volunteers is still an experiment, and an observational study on a random sample is still observational.

Association is not causation

  • Two variables are associated when they move together in the data.
  • One causes the other when changing it would change the other.
  • A lurking variable is a variable not included in the study that influences both the explanatory and the response variable.
  • Two variables are confounded when their effects on the response cannot be separated from each other.

Towns that sell more ice cream have more drownings. The association is real — it shows up in the data every year — but the causal story is wrong: hot weather drives both. A lurking variable does not create an illusion of association; it creates a real association that has the wrong explanation. An observational study alone cannot rule this out, because the groups being compared may differ in ways nobody measured.

Example: hormone therapy

For years, large observational studies found that women who took hormone therapy after menopause had much less heart disease. In the Nurses’ Health Study, which followed 59,337 women, current users of estrogen plus progestin had a relative risk of major coronary heart disease of 0.39 compared with non-users (Grodstein et al., 1996). Millions of women were prescribed hormones, in part to protect their hearts.

The Women’s Health Initiative then randomly assigned 16,608 women aged 50 to 79 to estrogen plus progestin or a placebo. In 2002 the trial was stopped early, after an average of 5.2 years, because the risks outweighed the benefits: invasive breast cancer had crossed its stopping boundary, and the hormone group had more coronary heart disease, not less (hazard ratio 1.29) (Writing Group for the WHI Investigators, 2002).

Why did the observational studies point the wrong way? Women who chose hormone therapy tended to be wealthier, better educated and healthier to begin with. When the observational studies were pooled with adjustment for socioeconomic status, the apparent benefit disappeared (Humphrey, Chan and Sox, 2002). Random assignment balanced those lurking variables between the two groups automatically.

Can we claim cause without an experiment?

Sometimes an experiment is impossible or unethical — nobody can randomly assign people to smoke for thirty years. Yet we are confident that smoking causes lung cancer. The evidence was built from decades of observational studies, and it was persuasive because it had many features at once (Hill, 1965):

  • a strong association, found consistently in different populations and study designs;
  • a dose-response pattern — the more cigarettes, the higher the risk;
  • the right order in time — smoking comes before the cancer;
  • a plausible biological explanation, supported by laboratory evidence.

The 2014 Surgeon General’s report, marking fifty years since the first federal report on smoking, concluded that cigarette smoking and secondhand smoke cause at least 480,000 premature deaths a year in the United States, including about 127,700 lung-cancer deaths (U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, 2014). Without an experiment, a causal claim needs this much evidence. A single observational study is never enough.

The language of experiments

  • Experimental units are what the treatments are applied to; when they are people they are called subjects.
  • A factor is an explanatory variable the researcher controls. Its levels are the specific values it is set to.
  • A treatment is one combination of factor levels.

Example. The VITAL trial randomly assigned 25,871 U.S. adults in a placebo-controlled trial with two factors: vitamin D (2,000 IU a day, or a placebo) and marine omega-3 fatty acids (1 g a day, or a placebo). Each factor has 2 levels, so there are \(2 \times 2 = 4\) treatments, and each participant received one of them — two questions answered in one trial. Over a median of 5.3 years, neither vitamin D nor omega-3 lowered the rate of invasive cancer or of major cardiovascular events (Manson et al., 2019a; Manson et al., 2019b).

Principles of experimental design

  1. Control. Compare the treatment against a control group, so that the treatment is the only systematic difference between the groups.
  2. Randomize. Use chance to assign units to treatments. Randomization balances the lurking variables we know about and the ones we don’t — something no amount of careful matching can promise.
  3. Replicate. Put enough units in each group that chance differences between the groups average out.

A placebo is a fake treatment that looks exactly like the real one — a sugar pill, or a saline injection. It exists because of the placebo effect: people often improve simply because they believe they are being treated. In one study of a performance-enhancing drug, believing one had taken the substance improved performance almost as much as actually taking it (McClung and Collins, 2007, quoted in Section 1.4 of the textbook). In a blind experiment the subjects do not know which treatment they received; in a double-blind experiment, neither do the people who measure the response.

Example: the Pfizer–BioNTech COVID-19 vaccine trial

In 2020, 43,548 people aged 16 and older were randomly assigned, in equal numbers, to two doses of the Pfizer–BioNTech COVID-19 vaccine or two injections of a saline placebo. Participants and the staff who assessed them did not know who had received which.

Group COVID-19 cases from 7 days after dose 2
Vaccine 8
Placebo 162

Among the 36,523 participants with no sign of earlier infection. Source: Polack et al. (2020).

The design is why that comparison means something. The saline injection gives the control group the same experience as the vaccine group, so the difference in cases cannot come from people’s expectations. Randomization means the people who got the vaccine were not, say, the more cautious people who would have chosen it — a lurking variable that would otherwise be confounded with the vaccine. With both in place, a vaccine efficacy of about 95% can be credited to the vaccine.

Example: a placebo surgery

Arthroscopic knee surgery for osteoarthritis was common, and patients reported that it helped. Moseley and colleagues randomly assigned 180 patients to one of three groups: two real arthroscopic procedures (debridement or lavage), or placebo surgery — skin incisions and a simulated procedure, with no arthroscope inserted. Patients and the staff who assessed their outcomes were blinded. Over two years of follow-up, at no point did either surgery group report less pain or better function than the placebo group (Moseley et al., 2002). The benefit patients felt came from believing they had been treated — and without a placebo group and blinding, nobody would have known.

Three experimental designs

Completely randomized design

In a completely randomized design, every unit is assigned to a treatment completely at random. It is the simplest design; the COVID-19 vaccine trial above was a completely randomized design with two groups.

Randomized block design

A block is a group of units that are alike in a way expected to affect the response, formed before treatments are assigned. In a randomized block design, the random assignment is carried out separately inside each block, and treatments are compared within blocks.

If men and women respond differently to a treatment, a completely randomized design lets that difference inflate the variation within each treatment group. Blocking on sex removes it, so a real treatment effect is easier to see. Blocking is to experiments what stratifying is to sampling.

Matched pairs design

A matched pairs design is a block design in which every block is a pair of very similar units — twins, or two plots of land side by side. Within each pair, a coin flip decides which member gets which treatment. Alternatively, each subject can serve as their own pair, receiving both treatments in a random order.

Before-and-after measurements on the same people — blood pressure before and after an exercise program, say — are paired data too. But with no random order and no control group, anything else that changed between the two measurements is confounded with the program, so they are much weaker evidence than a randomized matched pairs experiment.

Practice: design the study

(Adapted from the textbook’s Try It 1.21.) You want to know whether texting slows how quickly a driver reacts when the car ahead brakes.

  • The explanatory variable is texting or not; the response variable is reaction time; the treatments are driving while texting and driving only.
  • Splitting drivers at random into a texting group and a driving-only group would work, but reaction times vary a lot from person to person. A matched pairs design is better: each driver does both, in a random order, and is compared with themselves.
  • The drivers cannot be blinded — they know when they are texting — but the person measuring reaction times can be.

What randomization buys you

Chance plays two different roles in a study, and they buy two different conclusions:

  • Random sampling lets us generalize from the sample to the population it was drawn from.
  • Random assignment lets us conclude cause and effect.

Most studies have only one of the two. A clinical trial of volunteers can establish that a vaccine works — but strictly, for people like those volunteers. A random-sample survey can describe a whole population — but not explain why its variables are related.

Ethics

Example: the Facebook emotional contagion experiment

For one week in January 2012, Facebook altered the News Feeds of 689,003 users: for some, posts with positive emotional content were less likely to appear; for others, posts with negative content were. The study was published in 2014. The paper stated that the research “was consistent with Facebook’s Data Use Policy, to which all users agree prior to creating an account on Facebook, constituting informed consent for this research” (Kramer, Guillory and Hancock, 2014).

The journal disagreed. Its Editorial Expression of Concern called it “a matter of concern that the collection of the data by Facebook may have involved practices that were not fully consistent with the principles of obtaining informed consent and allowing participants to opt out.” As a private company, Facebook was not bound by the federal rules for human-subjects research, and Cornell University’s review board decided that its researcher, who saw only results, was not directly engaged in human research, so no review was required (PNAS Expression of Concern, via Retraction Watch). Clicking “I agree” on a terms-of-service page is not informed consent — even for a well-designed experiment.

Honest data

Ethics also means honest data. An investigation of the social psychologist Diederik Stapel found that he had created and altered datasets; falsified data tainted more than 55 papers he authored and 10 Ph.D. dissertations he supervised (Section 1.4 of the textbook). Problems can be much smaller and still matter. The textbook’s Example 1.22 describes a researcher surveying households in a community who:

  • picks a block where they already know many of the neighbors — a convenience sample that is biased, and misleading to present as representative;
  • skips four houses where nobody was home and never returns — nonresponse that may leave out, for example, working families;
  • fills in four forms with answers copied from neighbors — fabricated data, which is never acceptable.

Why the rules exist

The modern rules grew out of abuses. In the U.S. Public Health Service’s Untreated Syphilis Study at Tuskegee (1932–1972), 399 Black men with syphilis were followed for decades without being told the study’s true purpose and without being offered penicillin once it became the treatment of choice (CDC). The public response led to the National Research Act of 1974 and the 1979 Belmont Report, which set out three principles (HHS Office of Research Integrity):

  • Respect for persons — people decide for themselves whether to take part.
  • Beneficence — do no harm; maximize possible benefits and minimize possible risks.
  • Justice — the burdens and benefits of research are shared fairly.

Research ethics today

  • Informed consent: subjects are told the purpose, procedures and risks of a study in advance, and can refuse or withdraw at any time.
  • Confidentiality: information about individuals is protected and never released in a form that identifies them.
  • Institutional Review Boards (IRBs) review studies involving human subjects before they begin. At the University of Idaho the IRB is overseen by the Office of Research Assurances, under FSH 5200, Human Subject Research.

Ethics applies to data you did not collect yourself, too. When you use a public dataset for your project, check that it contains no information that could identify individuals.
